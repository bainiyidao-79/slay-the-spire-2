Slay The Spire 2 Tips & Guide: Deckbuilding That Wins Runs

Slay the Spire 2 tips that actually win runs: deckbuilding principles, act-by-act game plans, scaling advice and the combat checklist strong players use.

The best Slay the Spire 2 tips come down to one idea: stop forcing decks and start letting your deck, your map, and your fight plans make decisions for you. Slay the Spire 2 is harder than the original on purpose — enemy scaling is brutal (Act 1 enemies hitting for mid-20s and up), and early coverage from long-time players calls the Necrobinder effectively easy mode with the Silent close behind, which tells you how punishing the rest of the roster feels. This guide collects the deckbuilding principles, act-by-act plans, and per-turn combat checklist that high-level play actually uses.

Know Your Character First

Every character has its own card pool and mechanics, and your card picks should serve the character you're playing:

Character HP Core mechanics
Ironclad 80 Strength scaling, self-healing, Exhaust synergy; his Burning Blood relic restores 6 HP after every fight
Silent 70 Poison stacking, Shiv generation, card draw, discard synergy
Defect 75 Channels Lightning, Frost, Dark, Glass, and Plasma Orbs; Focus scaling
Regent 75 Stars resource (permanent until spent, unlimited stockpiling), Forge mechanic
Necrobinder 66 Summons Osty, who blocks attacks as an extra HP layer; Doom, Ethereal, and summon archetypes

Let the Deck Build Itself

The single most important habit for new players: don't aim for a specific deck in Act 1. Take good cards that serve you immediately — area-of-effect attacks, high block skills — and start pivoting toward a specific build only at the end of Act 1 or the start of Act 2.

This matters more than it did in the first game because Act 1 in Slay the Spire 2 may be the hardest act in the series. With most starting decks you're putting up a maximum of 15 block against enemies that hit for 24 — so a hand full of late-game archetype cards that do nothing yet will get you killed before they come online.

The classic example is Ironclad's Taunt versus Hellraiser. Taunt is a 1-cost skill for 7 block and 1 Vulnerable — fine immediately, and it plugs into two Ironclad archetypes (block-into-damage decks and Vulnerable decks) right away. Hellraiser costs 2 and lets you play all your Strike cards for free, which is incredible — later, after you've cut Defends, added Strikes, and upgraded a pile of cards, and only if you draw it turn one. In Act 1, Taunt wins runs; Hellraiser wins runs three acts from now. Take the card that's good today and let the deck form around it.

Learn Your Character's Deck Archetypes

Almost every card you can add synergizes with other cards, and character-specific archetypes become visible once you notice cards that share an effect type — the Silent, for instance, has Sly cards, Shiv cards, and Poison cards.

Start with a single archetype, then learn to mix two. A straightforward Sly deck adds free discard enablers: Prepared (0-cost, draw a card then discard a card) and Calculated Gamble (0-cost, discard your hand and redraw the same number) — every discard triggers Sly effects for free. Then notice that some Shiv cards also discard: Storm of Steel discards your whole hand and replaces it with Shivs, Hidden Daggers discards two cards for two Shivs. Add Master Planner (permanently adds Sly to any skill you play) or Hand Trick (7 block, temporarily applies Sly to a skill), and you get real cross-archetype combos — Hand Trick Sly onto Hidden Daggers, discard it, and the Sly discards two more cards and gives you two Shivs.

If mixing gets confusing, that's normal. Learn the cards as you play, browse a card database like Mobalytics, or watch streamers — Baalorlord, Frost Prime, and Jorbs for expert play, or Northernlion, who plays slower and talks through his reasoning, which is more useful when you're starting out.

Plan the Fight, Not the Turn

The most common combat mistake is evaluating plays turn by turn instead of across the whole fight. Before every fight — really, throughout the run — you should be able to answer two questions: how does my deck attack, and how does my deck block? Those are your damage plan and block plan, and they should drive every play.

  • Efficiency compounds. Waiting one turn to play Shrug It Off instead of Defend is 8 block for 1 energy instead of 5 — 60% more efficient. Waiting for Bludgeon instead of striking is 77% more efficient. Sequencing Bash before your strikes so they hit a Vulnerable target can nearly double your damage across three turns with the same energy.
  • Damage plans per character (starting-deck versions): Ironclad sets up Bash into strikes; Defect plays Zap and Dual Casts it; Necrobinder plays Bodyguards early to store damage on Osty, or Pattern into Celestial Might (the Vigor makes it 8 damage x3 for 2 energy).
  • Block plans are the same idea on defense: big efficient cards (Flame Barrier), split attack-block cards like Mangle against multi-attackers, or synergistic setups — Feel No Pain plus Fiend Fire, Cool Headed plus Loop, Frost Orbs plus Focus.
  • Multiplier cards make your plan fire more often. A Headbutt that puts your Bludgeon back on top of your deck lets you cast it twice per deck cycle — nearly as valuable as a second copy of Bludgeon. Grave Blast does the same for block plans (e.g., replaying a Weak-applying defend). These look like niche cards to new players; they're plan accelerators, and they scale as your plan's key cards get better.

Two fight-level questions decide everything else: how long will this fight last, and what are its mechanics? In short fights, immediate impact beats peak power — skip setup cards, and if you keep dying in regular fights, you probably have too many powers and not enough immediate damage or block (Hyper Beam and Refract are the clean examples). In long fights against elites and bosses, taking 25 damage to set up your engine is usually correct. And check enemy mechanics: kill a slime splitter when you still have attacks left for the spawned slimes; against an enemy that staggers at 70 HP and then winds up a ~39-damage hit, block harder once it's staggered.

The Per-Turn Combat Checklist

Ask these in order, every turn:

  1. Can I kill this enemy this turn? Check before anything else — a surprising number of fights are exact-lethal (one example: Ball Lightning + Dual Cast + Throwing Hatchet killing a 61 HP enemy that looked out of range). Also click your deck and ask can I kill next turn? — if yes, you can safely double-block now.
  2. Can a potion let me kill? Ending a fight one turn earlier is pure damage prevented.
  3. Can I die? If the incoming attack is beyond lethal, block first.
  4. What should I target? Kill what you can actually kill; deal with anything threatening lethal; then kill whatever becomes the biggest problem later (the enemy charging a 30 hit). If it's still a tie, take the most damage-efficient kill that lines up with your attacks.

The Act-by-Act Game Plan

Act 1 — take damage and path for elites. Cards that do more than the base Strike's 6 damage (Defile, Headbutt, Perfected Strike, Flatten) come first: a 140 HP elite takes eight turns to kill with the unmodified starting deck, and it's hitting for 20 every turn. Benchmark: about 100 damage over three turns puts you in a good place. Aim for at least three elites in Act 1 — one elite is a failed act, because elites are your gold and relics for the late game. Path with fail-safes (never one forced line of combats), take a fire before your first elite, rest only below 50% HP (upgrading a damage card usually saves more health than resting), spend potions aggressively, and use shops for card removes (they stack) or relics (280–300 gold).

Act 2 — build scaling. Act 2 elites (~200 HP, ~30 damage hits) are arguably the hardest fights in the game, and dying here almost always means one thing: no scaling plan — a way to get stronger during a fight until you can dish out ~300 damage. Demon Form is the textbook answer; Thrash improves itself as you exhaust attacks; Echo Form double-casts everything; Banshee's Cry ramps from 6 cost toward free; and in-fight thinning (exhaust, or the Regent's Transform) removes dead draws mid-combat. Because you won't always see Demon Form, build an archetype library — several plans you know how to execute — and skip card rewards that don't fit whichever one you're assembling this run.

Act 3 — make bad draws impossible. If you die in Act 3, the mistake happened earlier; Act 3 should be a celebration walk. Your only job is variance removal: card removes for leftover Strikes and curses, hold potions for disaster hands (Gambler's Brew redraws an all-Strikes opener; Bottled Potential replaces your hand), and relics that fix draws — Pocket Watch (extra draws when you play 3 or fewer cards), Choice of the Paradox (retain a key card), Runic Pyramid (never discard your hand). Draw manipulation like Hologram, Headbutt, and Snap is far stronger than it looks.

Don't Make Your Deck Too Thin

Counterintuitive after the last section, but true: don't remove too many cards. Slay the Spire 2's elites and bosses throw far more status cards at you than the first game did, and thin decks amplify that.

  • Phrog Parasite (Act 1) puts three Infection cards into your discard pile every two turns. Infections deal 3 damage whenever they're in your hand and never exhaust — with a 12-card deck, three turns converts a third of your deck into damage.
  • The Entomancer (Act 2 elite) adds a Dazed card directly into your draw pile every time you hit it, while having a chunky health bar and dealing ~18–28 damage a turn. Multi-hit and thin decks can lose this fight before it starts.

Perfected Strike or Hellraiser decks that want Strikes are the exception. For everyone else, thin your deck with a margin for statuses.

It Gets Easier — If You Keep Playing

Two structural reasons to keep going. First, Ascension tiers unlock per character as you win, each adding a modifier (less healing between acts, less gold from chests, up to the two-boss finale of Ascension 10) — but they're optional, and there's no reason to rush them before you've unlocked most Epochs in the Timeline. Second, chase the "Kill 15 Bosses" and "Kill 15 Elites" Epochs with every character: 15 boss kills unlock that character's exclusive relics, some of which are run-defining — the Silent's Twisted Funnel (passive poison every turn), the Regent's Mini Regent (+1 Strength per turn whenever you spend a Star), and the Defect's Gold-Plated Cables (your rightmost orb triggers twice per turn). The game literally pays you to keep throwing yourself at bosses.

Once the fundamentals click, push them further with our build guides, study the end-of-act walls in the boss guide, and check where your favorite cards land in the tier list.
