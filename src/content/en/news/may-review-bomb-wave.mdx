The May review wave was the sequel's strangest yet — not because of what players were angry about, but because of what the data said about who was angry. Between **May 6 and 8**, thousands of negative reviews landed in a 48-hour burst; by **May 11**, the game's overall rating had slipped to "Mostly Negative," and Steam's own review-bomb detection reportedly **locked the review section** temporarily.

The anomaly, as coverage at the time detailed: a significant share of the burst came from **accounts that had owned the game for under 24 hours** — review patterns inconsistent with the long-running playerbase that had written the game's [95%-positive launch history](/news/launch-day-95-percent-positive). Steam's bomb-protection system exists precisely to filter this pattern: coordinated off-platform campaigns arriving as synchronized review floods.

The context was layered. The [consultant-credit controversy](/news/consultant-controversy-reviews-slide) had primed the platforms, and the wave rode a broader grievance current rather than a single patch grievance — no 0.101-style rollback could address it, because there was no single change to roll back.

The practical takeaway for players reading ratings during the storm: Steam's historical review curves — which exclude detected bombs — kept the game's long-term score intact, and the developers' track record on [responding to legitimate feedback](/news/official-response-china-feedback) remained the more reliable signal of the game's direction than any 48-hour burst.
