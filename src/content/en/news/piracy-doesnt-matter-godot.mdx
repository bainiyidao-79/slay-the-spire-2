The engine-choice debate got its definitive answer from the source. In a Reddit thread on Slay the Spire 2's Godot underpinnings, a user raised the standard concern: open engines are easy to reverse-engineer, which invites cracks, cheats and knock-off builds. Mega Crit programmer **Jake Card's** response closed the thread's argument: the team understood all of that when it chose Godot — and **doesn't care.**

The full position, consistent with everything the studio had said since the [engine switch announcement](/news/slay-the-spire-2-switches-to-godot-engine):

- **The piracy math is a wash.** Pirates aren't lost sales; anti-piracy spend doesn't convert them, it just taxes paying customers with friction.
- **The upside compounds.** Godot's openness is precisely what powers the sequel's modding scene — the [mod loader](/tech/how-to-install-mods), the [console](/tech/console-commands), the community tools — plus engine transparency that lets players inspect and trust the game they're climbing.
- **The alternative was the problem.** Unity's 2024 fee controversy is why the studio moved; locking the game down again to spite pirates would have rebuilt the exact trust problem the switch solved.

Coming off a [three-million-copy launch week](/news/three-million-first-week-roadmap), the stance landed less as bravado than as evidence: the best-selling roguelike launch ever, cracked within days, visibly unharmed — while its mod community thrived. The market had, once again, agreed with Mega Crit's risk assessment.
