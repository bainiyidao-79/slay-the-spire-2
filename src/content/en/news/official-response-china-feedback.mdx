One day after the review-bomb headlines, Mega Crit did the thing that turned the story around: a detailed public response on the beta feedback storm — addressing Chinese players' concerns directly and laying out, in specific terms, how balance decisions actually get made.

The three-input framework the studio committed to:

1. **Real player feedback** — with a pointed endorsement of the game's built-in **F2 feedback tool**: the first-hand reports submitted directly from inside the game are "the most direct and valuable" input, and get priority review.
2. **Backend data** — win rates, pick rates and usage patterns at scale, so nerfs target measured outliers rather than loud anecdotes.
3. **Original design intent** — the guardrail that keeps balance patches from sanding off the game's identity.

The message explicitly acknowledged the Chinese community's concerns — the epicenter of the [review-bomb wave](/news/infinite-nerf-china-review-bomb) — and framed the Early Access contract as a two-way commitment: the beta branch exists for experimentation, and reversals are an acceptable outcome when experiments fail.

The proof followed within days. The [0.101 beta patch](/news/0-101-patch-reverts-nerfs) rolled back the most-criticized changes — with developer Anthony Giovannetti publicly crediting "a certain Chinese player's F2 feedback" for the reversal of a key Silent card change. The episode is now cited as a model of Early Access community management: storm, response, reversal, and a feedback channel players actually trust.
